Many people outside my narrow professional milieu often tell me they do not really understand what I do. Someone observed that others may assume that people like me lie about it. "From our parents who still can’t explain to their friends what their 40-year-old son does for a living, to those who still think we’re “in marketing,” we might as well be spies." French advertising legend Jacque Séguéla famously mused: "Don't tell my mother that I work in advertising; she believes I play the piano in a brothel. 

Indeed, my own parents never understood what I do. To be fair, I am the one to blame for that. On one side, I was never entirely sure myself. I found my way through work I was attracted to (rather than *a job*), and I changed roles a number of times. On the other, I never did a very good job of expressing it. Quite the irony for someone whose job could be described as helping others do just that.

When people ask me, I try to be clear without boring them with details: I help people clarify why they do what they do, how they do it, and why it matters. I worked as a designer, and now I am a brand strategist. But the words [[Design]], [[Brand]] and [[Strategy]] are very broad terms needing qualification.

You can find a very large number of agencies and consultants offering to *design your brand*, devise your *strategy* and perform a plethora of related activities. But what do they mean in practice? My parents would be none the wiser.

Design is easier; the results are tangible, visible assets. A logo, an ad, a product, a website. Even so, it is easy to mix up design (as a skill) with the design process (problem solving) with the design execution (the production of assets).

Strategy and Brand are even more abstract concepts, discussed at length in innumerable texts by as many distinguished experts, and a considerable number of snake-oil salesmen. You'll be the judge, but I hope you'll place me in the first and not the second group.

I am writing this to clarify my story, how I answer questions about 'what I do', but also to reflect on what the work entails.
Fellow strategist [Alex Morris](https://alexmorris.co/) wrote, "To me, writing a strategy isn't a paint-by-the-numbers kind of thing. There are end deliverables, and there are fundamentals that help you get there, but it's more exploration than anything". Unlike myself, Alex works with strategy in the context of advertising, but the same principles apply to what I do.

There are many 'paint by numbers' approaches to brand strategy development, and some agencies even feel compelled to develop a 'proprietary' method: they list the steps involved, put some kind of spin and fancy terminology around them, and brand it as *OurProcess™️*. That's a sign. One thing I learned in 30+ years of doing this is that there is no one-size-fits-all formula for strategy. As Alex says, it's an exploration. And, yes, there are [[Brand Strategy Framework|deliverables and fundamentals]] but the journey is different every time.

Projects emerge from a combination of factors. Primarily a business need and a leadership decision. One or more people in a position of leadership see a challenge or an opportunity and decide to embark on an activity designed to build an advantage for their organisation. Sometimes this is due to a change in market conditions, the launch of a new initiative, the need to reach a new audience, or to land a new message. The list could go on; my point is that there are many variables, which is why every project is different. Not least because the people involved are different, even when other factors are the same. [[Brands start and end with people]]. The first step for any strategy is to talk to the right people to attempt a diagnosis of the 'problem' the work needs to solve. Richard Rumelt provides a perfect synthesis. 

> "The kernel of a strategy contains three elements: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent action" — Richard Rumelt, *[Good Strategy Bad Strategy](https://www.google.co.uk/books/edition/Good_Strategy_Bad_Strategy/nTU65JgxgOAC?hl=en&authuser=2&gbpv=0)*

That's it. The job of a strategy consultant is to help people in the client organisation formulate the right diagnosis, develop a guiding policy and orchestrate coherent action.

One day my cousin Fabrizio, CEO of a hotel group, asked me to have a chat about their brand. After an hour in which I mainly asked him questions, he said: "Now I get what you do: you are like a therapist for organisations." His insight captures a truth about the nature of brand strategy work. It is comparable to what a psychotherapist does with a client. A strategist helps people in organisations make sense of their thoughts, feelings, and behaviours to find balance and alignment. I help organisations understand their beliefs and values, make sense of their purpose and direction, identify patterns of behaviour, work toward strategic alignment, create frameworks for collective identity, and coordinate coherent communications.

The work requires talking to people—both individually (personal beliefs, motivations, goals) and collectively (shared values, vision, culture). Discovery happens through dialogue: asking questions to surface implicit beliefs, listening for patterns and tensions, reflecting back what we hear, helping articulate what matters.

The outcome is a [[Symbolic synthesis|system of symbols]] designed to facilitate a collective identity project. It establishes common ground around beliefs, values, vision, objectives, mission, and behaviours. It creates clarity about what makes the organisation unique and enables alignment toward common goals. This goes beyond a brand book or a new set of visual or verbal assets. It is a system that provides a framework for decision-making, helps people understand their role in the collective story, enables coherent action across the organisation, and supports the development of organisational culture.

My parents would still be nonplussed at all of this. As with many activities, the best way to understand it involves some direct experience. The people I work with have no trouble understanding what I do since we do it together. But for anyone else, I can offer some examples.
